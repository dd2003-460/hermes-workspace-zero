Universal calculation approach of overlap reduction function for
pulsar timing array and laser interferometer detector
Yu Hu , Pan-Pan Wang , Yu-Jie Tan , and Cheng-Gang Shao
*
National Gravitation Laboratory, MOE Key Laboratory of Fundamental Physical Quantities Measurement, and
School of Physics, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan 430074, People’s Republic of China
(Received 25 November 2024; revised 16 March 2025; accepted 7 April 2025; published 25 April 2025)
Stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) is one of the major targets for contemporary
gravitational wave (GW) observations, including pulsar timing array (PTA), ground-based laser
interferometer (GbLI), and space-based laser interferometer (SbLI). The data analysis of SGWB involves
cross-correlating the outputs of a pair of detectors to find the functional form of the correlations, which is
referred to as the overlap reduction function (ORF). While conventional approaches employ detector-
specific approximations for ORF calculations, in this paper, we present a universal formula to calculate the
precise ORF for isotropic SGWB detection with PTA and laser interferometer detectors. Since no
approximations are employed, the exact value of the ORF can be obtained for all three types of detectors.

I. INTRODUCTION
The increasing number of GWs from compact binary co-
alescences detected by LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA Collaboration
is opening the door to GW astronomy [1–4]. The successful
detection of GWs by ground-based laser interferometers has
accelerated the implementation of third-generation ground-
based laser interferometry detectors, such as Cosmic
Explorer [5] and Einstein Telescope [6], as well as space-
based laser interferometry detectors including LISA [7],
TianQin [8], Taiji [9], and DECIGO [10]. In addition, pulsar
timing arrays (PTAs) offer a complementary approach to
detect GWs at much lower frequenciesinthenanohertz range
[11–14]. A multiband GW detection network comprising
various types of detectors is being developed, which is of
greatsignificanceforthestudyofastronomy,cosmology,and
fundamental physics.
Stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) is
one of the primary targets of GW observations, the origins
of which can be divided mainly into astronomical and
cosmological divisions. The astronomical source is obser-
vationally well motivated, consisting of an incoherent
superposition of individually unresolved gravitational radi-
ation from a large number of compact binary coalescences
[15–19]. There are numerous cosmological sources that can
generate SGWB, such as inflation [20,21], cosmic strings
[22–24], first-order phase transitions [25–27], and domain
walls [28–32]. The noiselike characteristics and large
uncertainties associated with SGWB make it difficult to
detect. No evidence for the existence of SGWB has
been found in the data from the first three observation
run (O1-O3) of Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo, and
an upper limit on the dimensionless energy density for an
isotropic SGWB of ΩGW ≤5.8 × 10−9 at hertz band is
derived [33]. Recently, evidence for the existence of nano-
hertz SGWB in the data of different PTA organizations has
been announced at varying levels of significance [34–37].
However, the significance of the signal is not high enough to
confirm that it is indeed from a SGWB, and the information
of its source remains undetermined.
There is no waveform model for SGWB, so the detection
of SGWB is implemented by searching for information
about its statistical characteristics. SGWB cannot be dis-
tinguished from the instrumental noise in a single detector,
but are correlated between a pair of detectors, which differ
from the uncorrelated noise. Cross-correlation analysis is a
typical method to detect SGWB, which is based on the
correlation of the outputs of a pair of detectors [38,39]. The
correlation is determined by the intensity of SGWB and
the geometric factors of the detector pair including the
relative orientation, the separation and the difference
configuration of the detectors. The geometric factor can
be quantified by the overlap reduction function (ORF)
[38,40–43], which is defined as the form of the average of
the product of the response functions of the two detectors
over the sky map of SGWB.
In general, the integration of ORF has to be calculated
numerically, due to the nontrivial frequency-dependent
exponential terms. However, different approximations can
be adopted to simplify the calculation for various types of
detectors, depending on the relationship between the wave-
length of the GWs and the arm lengths of the detectors. For
PTAs, the ORF has been calculated analytically using the
short-wavelength approximation (where the ratio of the

similarities between space-based detectors and those on
the ground, as both are laser interferometric types and
the wavelengths of the GWs are about an order of

be employed to suppress laser phase noise for space-based

Michelson-type TDI channels can be calculated with the
same small-antenna approximation for a pair of triangle
space-based detectors [48,49]. In fact, ORFs of any TDI
channels can be calculated by dividing the TDI channel
into individual links, with results found in [50], where the
small-antenna approximation is extended to the second
order for higher accuracy.
In this paper, we present a universal calculation approach
of ORF for SGWB detection with PTA and laser interfer-
ometer detector. The idea of this formula is as follows:
(1) express GW response function of the detector as linear
combinations according to fundamental one-way path,
(2) decompose the ORF of the detector pair into a combi-
nation of ORFs between different one-way links, and
(3) calculate the ORFs between the different one-way links
separately and add them together. The breakthrough lies in
our analytic treatment of the ORF between two one-way
links. By selecting proper coordinate system to simplify
integrand, transforming the azimuth integral variable into a
complex plane and then employing residue theorem, we
resolve this traditionally intractable integral. Adopting this
new method, we calculate the ORF for PTA, and the result is
consistent with the analytic expression in [45]. We also
calculate the ORF for ground-based and space-based laser
interferometer, and the results are in good agreement with
the numerical integration.
The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we briefly
describe the cross-correlation analysis of SGWB and
present the definition of the ORF for different types of
detectors. In Sec. III, we summarize the commonalities of
the ORFs of PTA and laser interferometer detectors that can
be expressed as the ORFs of two one-way links or a
combination of them, and calculate the ORFs for two
general one-way links. In Sec. IV, we apply the method to
calculate the ORFs for PTA, ground-based and space-based
laser interferometer detectors. Finally, in Sec. V, we present
our concluding remarks.

III. OVERLAP REDUCTION FUNCTION
The overlap reduction function is defined as the average
of the product of the response functions of a pair of detectors
over the sky map of SGWB, which quantifies the sensitivity
of cross-correlation statistics between the two detectors to
the SGWB. The intensity of the correlated signal between
two detectors caused by SGWB is determined by the degree
of correlation between two detectors, which is characterized
by the ORF. This correlation strength depends critically on
two geometric factors: the separation distance between two
detectors and their relative orientation. The separation and
nonalignment of two detectors diminish the sensitivity of the
cross-correlation statistics. The ORF quantifies the degree of
overlap between two detectors, while also including the
reduction effect due to geometric misalignment. Beyond
the geometric factors, detector design differences further
influence correlation sensitivity, which is reflected in the
ORF. Of course, it is not meaningful to perform cross-
correlation analysis between two detectors of a completely
different type, such as PTA and GbLI, because their sensitive
frequency bands do not have any intersection. However,
even for similar classes like SbLI, variations in arm lengths
has a non-negligible impact on the ORF through frequency-
dependent response differences.
The ORF acts as a transfer function relating the SGWB
power spectrum to the expected cross-correlation power
spectrum of the two detectors. Efficient and accurate
calculation of the ORF is crucial for practical data analysis.
It is a function of the frequency of the GWs, and the
frequency dependence of the ORF is determined by the
response functions of the detectors. Due to the differences
in the observational principles for different types of
detectors, their corresponding response functions exhibit
different forms, as shown in Eq. (6). In this way, the ORF of
different types of detectors shows different behaviors, and
appropriate approximation methods can be employed to
calculate the corresponding ORF. Interested readers can
refer to the review [43] for a more detailed discussion and
information on general ORF for SGWB with alternative
polarization, anisotropy and asymmetry.
In this paper, we present a unified formula to calculate
the ORF for isotropic SGWB detection using PTA and laser
interferometer detectors. Although these two types of
detectors are fundamentally different in terms of their
physical principles and detection methods, their basic
constituent elements are the same, namely, the one-way
link. A one-way link represents a one-way signal path
between two spatial points, originating from one location
and propagating to another. Pulsar timing experiments
naturally establish one-way links between pulsars and
Earth. Ground-based L-shaped interferometers operate by
emitting a laser from the beam splitter at the origin along
two different paths, propagating the same distance to the
end mirrors, and then reflecting back to the origin for
interference. Therefore, a ground-based detector can be
regarded as the difference between two round-trip links,
while a round-trip link is a combination of two one-way
links in opposite directions with a chronological order.
Furthermore, a space-based detector consists of three
satellites and six one-way links connecting them, and
any TDI channel is a linear combination of these six
one-way links. Consequently, the response function of both
PTAs and laser interferometer detectors can be expressed as
a combination of the one-way link response functions. The
ORF of these different types of detectors can each be
decomposed into ORFs between two one-way links.

V. CONCLUDING REMARKS
SGWB is an important GW target for detectors in various
frequency bands. The cross-correlation analysis of a pair of
detectors is an effective way to search for SGWB. The ORF
is a key factor in the cross-correlation analysis, which
quantifies the sensitivity of the correlation of detector
pair to the SGWB. There are different approximations to
deal with the calculation of ORFs for different types of
detectors. In this paper, we have provided a general formula
to calculate the ORF for searching isotropic SGWB with
PTA and laser interferometer detectors. A pair of pulsar in
PTA is just two one-way links with the same end point. The
laser interferometer detector can be regarded as a combi-
nation of many one-way links. This means that as long as
the ORF of a pair of general one-way links can be
calculated, the ORF of any type of detector composed of
links can be calculated. We use the integral variable
transformation and the residue theorem to derive the exact
analytic expression of the ORF for two one-way links.
Since no approximation is used in the derivation, the result
is accurate for any frequency range. This result can be
applied directly to different types of detectors to obtain an
exact expression of the ORF.
We use the formula to calculate the ORF for various pairs
of detectors, including PTA, GbLI, and SbLI. The analyti-
cal result of ORF for PTA is consistent with the result
obtained by other method [45]. The ORF for GbLI can be
calculated by the small-antenna approximation [40–42],
which is valid for a wide range of frequencies where GbLIs
are sensitive. With the increase of frequency, the error of the
small-antenna approximation increases gradually. The ORF
of Einstein Telescope in Fig. 5 clearly illustrated this for
third-generation detectors whose high-frequency sensitivity
is not negligible. The ORF for SbLI can likewise be

The ORF for LISA-TianQin and LISA-Taiji in Fig. 6 shows

accurate enough for high frequency range and the analytical
approach in this paper significantly improves this situation.
Although the formula we propose is general to calculate
the ORF for different type of detector, the significance for
different detectors is different for the calculation require-
ments in actual data analysis. First of all, for PTA, the
actual observed pulsar is far enough away from the Earth,
and therefore the short-wavelength approximation accuracy
is sufficient in target frequency range ½10−9; 10−7 Hz.
From the analysis in [45], the analytical formula only
improves the accuracy for the pulsar pairs that are very
close together or close to the Earth. These conditions are
rare among the currently observed pulsars. Second, for
GbLI, the sensitive frequency range to detect SGWB is
½101; 102 Hz, and the detector arm length is on the order of
kilometers, which ensures that the small-antenna approxi-
mation, β ¼ 2πfL=c ≪1, is valid in this frequency range.
The future third-generation detectors, such as Einstein
Telescope, have a higher sensitivity, and the arm length
is on the order of tens of kilometers, which means wider
detection frequency range (up to 103 Hz) and insufficient
accuracy of the small-antenna approximation in high
frequency range. Finally, for SbLI, the sensitive frequency
range is ½10−4; 10−1 Hz, and the arm length is on the order
of 109 kilometers, which indicates that the small-antenna
approximation is not valid in high frequency range, for
example, β ¼ 2πfL=c ∼1 for f ∼10−1 Hz. Among these
different detectors, the analytical method proposed in this
paper is most useful for SbLI, which can significantly
improve the accuracy of the ORF calculation in high
frequency range.
The original definition of the ORF is an integral over
two-dimensional sphere. Rather than looking for analytical
results, numerical integration is a more direct way to
calculate the ORF. However, as the frequency increases,
the oscillations of the integrand intensify, and thus the
number of points required for numerical integration
increases rapidly, which makes the calculation time-
consuming. Table I shows the comparison of time and
accuracy to calculate ORF with different methods for PTA,
GbLI, and SbLI. All calculations are performed using
Mathematica. For PTA, the numerical integration is diffi-
cult to achieve high accuracy, especially for high frequency,
due to that βu and βv are very large across the frequency
range. As for GbLI, only at high frequency, the numerical
integration takes significantly longer time than the analyti-
cal method. Similarly, the numerical integration is time-
consuming for high frequency range of SbLI. Compared
with the other two type of detectors, in whose respective
frequency range the approximate analytical method is
sufficient accurate and efficient, the extended small-
antenna approximation for SbLI is not accurate enough
and the numerical integration is particularly time-consum-
ing. In the actual data analytic of SbLI, the relative
orientation of two detectors in their respective orbits is
changing with time, and the time-varying ORF needs to be
calculated repeatedly. In general, the analytical calculation
approach for ORF proposed in this paper is of great
significance for the actual data analysis of SbLI.
The formula we propose is intuitive and does not have
any approximations, so it has strong applicability and
convenience of extension. The general metric theories of
gravity predict the existence of alternative polarization
modes of GWs. Furthermore, anisotropy, linear and circular
polarization of SGWB may arise in specific models of
SGWB. We leave the study of these extension to
future work.
